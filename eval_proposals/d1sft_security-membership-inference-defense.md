Let me start from the invariant I trust most: a membership inference attack succeeds because train and non-train examples leave the model's internal statistics in different regimes. The attack reads off a single scalar (confidence, loss) per example, so the *difference* between train and non-train is the signal. Any defense that collapses that difference while keeping the model predictive is a candidate.

The standard story is: shrink the train-test gap by regularizing predictions. But I don't think shrinking the gap is the right lever. A regularizer that makes the model less confident shrinks the train-test gap, but it also shrinks the non-train test gap, and that's the crux. The attack is not comparing two train examples — it's comparing one train example to many non-train examples. So if the defense makes non-train examples look *more* like train examples, the attack's signal gets amplified, not muted. That's the paradox I keep coming back to.

What transfers cleanly from adversarial training is the idea of contrasting: train with its own batch, and non-train with a fixed, independent batch. But I can't just add a contrastive loss — the contrastive objective is a ratio over the entire batch, which is hard to differentiate and tends to explode. So I need to express it in a form that a gradient-based optimizer can handle.

Here's the transfer I want to make concrete. The adversarial training objective for a batch is often written as: minimize over (x,y) in the batch of `max(0, margin - y·logits)` and maximize over (x',y') in a separate batch of `max(0, y'·logits - margin)`. That's the adversarial margin objective. I'll borrow the *shape* of that, not the specific adversarial examples. Instead of adversarial examples, I'll use: the current training batch (x,y) and a *fixed, independent batch* (x',y') from the same dataset but a different split.
