Let me start from what I actually trust, not from the literature consensus. When I look at the objective — preserve both local neighborhoods and global structure — the natural instinct is 'maximize one, then regularize the other.' That's exactly what t-SNE does: optimize perplexity to preserve local neighborhoods, then rely on the KL divergence loss to keep some global structure. But the whole reason t-SNE is so famous is that its global structure is famously poor. So the framing itself is suspect.

What if the problem isn't regularization strength but the object being regularized? In t-SNE, the regularization is on the *distance* between points. If distance is the thing you're regularizing, it's not surprising that global structure gets lost — the optimizer can move points far apart to satisfy local constraints, and the only thing stopping it is the penalty on moving them far. That's an arms race. A distance penalty is the wrong regularization target.

So let me reframe it as a constraint rather than a penalty. Instead of saying 'try to keep points close while penalizing moving them,' say 'points that are neighbors in the original space must be neighbors in the embedding.' That reframes the whole optimization: now the optimizer can't cheat by moving points far away, because the constraint stops it. It forces the optimizer to find a solution where both local and global structure hold simultaneously, instead of trying to balance them.

How do I make that concrete? I want a mechanism where 'neighborhoods are preserved' is a hard constraint, not a soft penalty. One way to enforce such a constraint is through a quadratic program: minimize reconstruction error subject to pairwise distance constraints. Specifically, for each pair of points that are neighbors in the original space, add a constraint that their distance in the embedding cannot exceed some threshold. The threshold is the key — it should be the distance of the k-th nearest neighbor in the original space, so that the k-nearest-neighbor structure is preserved exactly. That makes it a constrained optimization over the low-dimensional coordinates Y: minimize ||X - f(Y)||^2 subject to d(Y_i, Y_j) ≤ d(X_i, X_k) for all i,j that are neighbors, where d(X_i, X_k) is the distance to the k-th neighbor.

Now the crux: why does this cut against the consensus, and why does it plausibly work? The consensus view is that preserving local structure and global structure are fundamentally competing objectives, and the best you can do is balance them. My claim is that the competition is an artifact of using a distance-based penalty — switch to a distance-based *constraint* and the competition disappears. The non-trivial part is that the constraint must be on the k-th neighbor, not the first or second — any smaller k would allow the optimizer to trivially satisfy the constraint by clustering everything, and any larger k would relax the constraint too much. The constraint needs to be tight enough to preserve structure but loose enough to allow a valid solution to exist.

One caveat I have to watch: this formulation is quadratic in the number of constraints, so it will scale poorly with dataset size. For large datasets, I'd need to sample constraints or approximate the k-th neighbor threshold rather than compute it exactly. But on the scale of 5000 samples, a full QP should be feasible.

Core idea: Reframe nonlinear dimensionality reduction as a constrained optimization — minimize reconstruction error subject to a hard distance constraint that points which are neighbors in the original space must be neighbors in the embedding, enforced up to the k-th nearest neighbor distance.

Non-trivial crux: The constraint must be on the k-th neighbor distance, not a smaller k (which would allow trivial clustering) nor a larger k (which would relax the constraint too much); and this cuts against the consensus by claiming that the apparent tension between local and global structure is an artifact of distance penalties, not inherent.
